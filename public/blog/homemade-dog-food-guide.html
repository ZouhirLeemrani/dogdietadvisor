Homemade Dog Food: The Complete Vet-Approved Guide

The appeal is real — you know exactly what goes in the bowl. But most homemade dog food diets fail silently. Here's how to do it right, and when to stick with commercial food.

🐾 DogDietAdvisor 📅 May 15, 2026 ⏱ 10 min read
Home› Blog› Homemade Dog Food Guide

The homemade dog food trend has exploded in 2026 — driven by pet food recalls, ingredient transparency concerns, and the general "humanization" of dogs. The appeal is understandable. You know exactly what's in the bowl. But the reality, as veterinary nutritionists will tell you, is that the vast majority of homemade dog food diets are nutritionally incomplete — and the consequences don't show up for months or years.

This guide gives you the honest picture: what the research says, what a properly balanced recipe actually requires, which ingredients to avoid, and when homemade is genuinely worth the effort.

⚠️ The critical caveat upfrontA study published in the Journal of Nutritional Science found that over 95% of homemade dog food recipes found online were nutritionally deficient in at least one essential nutrient — most commonly calcium, zinc, vitamin D, and omega-3 fatty acids. Before committing to homemade feeding, consult a board-certified veterinary nutritionist (DACVN).

Why Homemade Dog Food Is Genuinely Difficult to Get Right

Dogs need over 40 essential nutrients in precise ratios. Unlike humans — who eat varied diets across multiple meals — a dog eating the same homemade recipe every day gets exactly what that recipe provides, and nothing else. A deficiency in calcium causes bone disease. A deficiency in iodine causes thyroid dysfunction. A copper deficiency causes coat and liver problems. None of these show symptoms until significant damage has occurred.

Commercial pet food brands that meet AAFCO standards have achieved this balance through feeding trials and laboratory analysis. Replicating it at home requires the same rigor — which means actual recipe formulation by a nutritionist, not a blog post from 2019.

Who Should Consider Homemade Dog Food

Homemade feeding makes the most sense in these situations:

  • Dogs with multiple confirmed food allergies where no commercial formula avoids all triggers — a veterinary nutritionist can formulate a single novel protein recipe precisely
  • Dogs with specific medical conditions (kidney disease, liver disease, pancreatitis) where a veterinary nutritionist works alongside your vet to formulate therapeutic nutrition
  • Owners with the time and commitment to follow a nutritionist-formulated recipe exactly, including supplements, every day without shortcuts

For most healthy dogs with no specific medical needs, a high-quality commercial food from a brand with veterinary nutritionists on staff — Purina Pro Plan, Hill's Science Diet, Royal Canin — provides better, more consistent nutrition than most homemade alternatives.

A Properly Balanced Homemade Dog Food: The Structure

A nutritionist-formulated homemade diet typically follows this approximate structure by weight (before cooking):

  • 50% protein — named animal muscle meat (chicken, beef, turkey, salmon). Organ meat (liver, kidney) typically 10–15% of the protein portion for micronutrients
  • 25% carbohydrate — cooked rice, sweet potato, oats, or barley. Provides energy and digestible fiber
  • 25% vegetables — cooked carrots, green beans, broccoli, spinach, zucchini. Provides fiber, vitamins, antioxidants
  • Supplements — calcium, fish oil, vitamin/mineral premix formulated to AAFCO standards

📋 The supplement non-negotiableThe single most common mistake in homemade dog food is omitting or underdosing the calcium supplement. Dogs eating meat-based diets without adequate calcium develop nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism — the body leaches calcium from bones to maintain blood levels. This is irreversible by the time symptoms appear. A calcium supplement is not optional.

Ingredients to Avoid in Homemade Dog Food

These are toxic or harmful to dogs and must never appear in homemade recipes:

  • Onions and garlic (all forms — raw, cooked, powder) — cause hemolytic anemia
  • Grapes and raisins — cause acute kidney failure, mechanism unknown, dose threshold also unknown
  • Macadamia nuts — neurological toxicity
  • Xylitol — causes severe hypoglycemia; found in some peanut butters and baked goods
  • Chocolate — theobromine toxicity
  • Cooked bones — splinter and cause internal injuries. Raw bones carry bacterial risk
  • Avocado — persin in the flesh can cause vomiting and diarrhea
  • Raw salmon (Pacific Northwest) — can carry Neorickettsia helminthoeca, which is fatal if untreated
  • Nutmeg — myristicin toxicity
  • Corn on the cob — the cob causes intestinal obstruction

Getting a Recipe Formulated by a Nutritionist

The only safe way to feed homemade long-term is with a recipe formulated for your specific dog by a board-certified veterinary nutritionist (Diplomate ACVN). Two reliable services:

  • BalanceIT.com — provides supplement formulas and recipe balancing tools developed by veterinary nutritionists. You choose the protein and carbohydrate; they provide the exact supplement to balance it.
  • ASPCA Animal Poison Control's nutritionist referral network — connects you with board-certified veterinary nutritionists who can formulate recipes for dogs with complex medical needs

Expect to pay $200–400 for a professional consultation. That's significantly less than the veterinary bills for nutritional deficiency disease.

The Mixing Approach: A Practical Middle Ground

For owners who want to incorporate fresh food without the risk of full homemade feeding, mixing is a practical compromise. Add fresh food as a topper to a complete commercial food — cooked chicken, vegetables, eggs — and keep it under 25% of total daily calories. This way the commercial food provides the nutritional foundation, and fresh food adds palatability and variety without creating deficiencies.

This approach avoids the primary risk of homemade feeding (nutritional incompleteness) while still giving you control over a portion of your dog's diet. See our dry vs wet food guide for how mixing works in practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is homemade dog food better than commercial dog food?

Not automatically — and often the opposite. High-quality commercial foods from companies with veterinary nutritionists on staff (Purina Pro Plan, Hill's Science Diet, Royal Canin) are rigorously tested and nutritionally complete. Most homemade diets prepared without professional formulation are nutritionally deficient. Homemade food prepared with a nutritionist-formulated recipe and proper supplements can be excellent — but it requires significantly more effort and knowledge than most owners realize.

How do I know if my homemade dog food recipe is balanced?

The only reliable way is to have the recipe analyzed by a board-certified veterinary nutritionist or through a service like BalanceIT.com. Recipes found online — even from veterinary websites — are frequently incomplete. Don't rely on appearance, ingredient quality, or your dog's short-term health to judge nutritional adequacy. Many deficiencies take months or years to become visible.

Can I feed my dog cooked chicken and rice every day?

Short-term, yes — chicken and rice is a standard bland diet for dogs recovering from digestive illness. Long-term, no. Chicken and rice alone is severely deficient in calcium, vitamin D, zinc, iodine, and essential fatty acids. Feeding it daily without a complete supplement will cause nutritional disease within months. It must be supplemented to AAFCO standards before it can serve as a complete diet.

What vegetables are safe for dogs in homemade food?

Safe and nutritious vegetables include: carrots, green beans, broccoli (in moderation), peas, sweet potato (cooked), zucchini, cucumber, spinach (in moderation — high oxalates), and pumpkin. Cook all vegetables for dogs — raw vegetables are harder to digest and some cooking-sensitive vitamins are actually more bioavailable after light cooking. Avoid onions, garlic, chives, leeks, grapes, raisins, and avocado.

Do I need to add supplements to homemade dog food?

Yes — always. At minimum, a homemade diet requires a calcium supplement, a vitamin/mineral premix formulated to AAFCO standards, and fish oil for omega-3 fatty acids. The exact doses depend on the specific recipe, your dog's size, and life stage. Using a generic "multivitamin" is not sufficient — the amounts must be calculated for your specific recipe. A veterinary nutritionist or BalanceIT.com can provide exact supplement recommendations.

The Bottom Line

Homemade dog food can be nutritionally excellent — but only when formulated correctly. The barrier is real: you need professional recipe formulation, precise supplementation, and consistent preparation. For most healthy dogs, a high-quality commercial food is the safer, more practical choice. For dogs with specific allergies or medical conditions, homemade food formulated by a veterinary nutritionist can be the best option available. The worst outcome — and the most common one — is feeding an "instinct-based" homemade diet without professional input and discovering the damage months later.
